# Brake Systems – Sample Notes

## How hydraulic brakes work
Pressing the brake pedal pushes a piston in the master cylinder. That pressure travels through the brake lines as brake fluid, which does not compress. At each wheel, the fluid pushes pistons in the caliper (disc brakes) or wheel cylinder (drum brakes), which press friction material against a rotating surface and slow the wheel.

## Disc brakes
A disc brake has a rotor that spins with the wheel, a caliper that holds the brake pads, and the pads themselves. When the caliper squeezes the pads against the rotor, friction turns the vehicle's motion into heat.

- Most modern cars use disc brakes on the front wheels, because the front brakes do most of the stopping work.
- Rotors should be measured against the minimum thickness stamped on the rotor. A rotor below minimum thickness must be replaced, not resurfaced.

## Drum brakes
A drum brake uses curved brake shoes inside a drum that turns with the wheel. The wheel cylinder pushes the shoes outward against the drum. Drum brakes are still common on the rear wheels of some trucks and economy cars, and the drum often doubles as the parking brake.

## Brake fluid
- Brake fluid is hygroscopic: it absorbs moisture from the air over time.
- Water in the fluid lowers its boiling point. Under hard braking the fluid can boil, causing a soft or sinking pedal called brake fade.
- Always use the fluid type listed on the master cylinder cap (for example DOT 3 or DOT 4). DOT 5 is silicone-based and must not be mixed with DOT 3 or DOT 4.

## Common symptoms
| Symptom | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Squealing when braking | Wear indicator touching the rotor; pads are low |
| Grinding noise | Pads worn to the backing plate, metal on metal |
| Pulsing pedal | Uneven rotor thickness (often called a warped rotor) |
| Soft or spongy pedal | Air in the lines or moisture in the fluid |
| Car pulls to one side | Stuck caliper or uneven pad wear |
